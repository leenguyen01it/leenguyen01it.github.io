A Wall of Green

The Sahel is a long, narrow region that stretches across Africa just south of the Sahara Desert. For centuries, its people have farmed and herded animals on land that receives little and unpredictable rain. In recent decades, droughts, population growth and the loss of trees have caused much of this land to become less productive, a process known as land degradation. In some places, the soil has become so poor that farming is almost impossible.

In 2007, the African Union launched an ambitious response: the Great Green Wall. The original idea was simple and striking – to plant a belt of trees around 15 kilometres wide and nearly 8,000 kilometres long, running from Senegal on the Atlantic coast to Djibouti on the Indian Ocean. The trees, it was hoped, would hold back the advance of the desert, protect the soil and provide food and income for local communities. The project set a target of restoring 100 million hectares of land by 2030.

Progress has been slower than its supporters hoped. A report published around the middle of the project's timeline estimated that only a small fraction of the target had been achieved, and that funding had fallen far short of what was needed. In some countries, millions of trees were planted, but many of them died because they were the wrong species for the local conditions, or because nobody was responsible for watering and protecting them after planting.

These difficulties led to an important change in thinking. Instead of a single continuous line of trees, the Great Green Wall is now generally understood as a mosaic of different land restoration projects, designed with local communities and suited to local conditions. Experts also realised that some of the most successful examples of restoration in the Sahel had involved little or no tree planting at all.

In southern Niger, for example, farmers discovered that the roots of trees cut down years earlier were often still alive beneath their fields. By protecting the young shoots that grew from these roots and pruning them carefully, they were able to regrow trees at very low cost. This approach, known as farmer-managed natural regeneration, has spread across millions of hectares. The trees provide shade, firewood and animal feed, and their leaves improve the soil.

In Burkina Faso, farmers revived a traditional technique known as zaï. During the dry season, they dig small pits in hard, crusted ground and fill them with manure or compost. When the rains arrive, the pits collect water and hold it close to the plants' roots. Seeds of crops such as millet or sorghum are then planted in the pits. The organic material attracts termites, whose tunnels help water to enter the soil more deeply. Over several years, fields that had been abandoned can become productive again.

Supporters of the Great Green Wall argue that its greatest achievement may be to have drawn international attention to the problem of land degradation. Critics respond that attention is not enough, and that far more money and long-term commitment will be required if the project is to meet its goals.

Cities That Cook

A On a summer evening, the centre of a large city can be several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside. This phenomenon, known as the urban heat island, was first described in the nineteenth century, when an amateur scientist recorded that London was consistently warmer than nearby villages. Today, with more than half of the world's population living in cities and global temperatures rising, it has become a serious concern for public health.

B Several factors combine to create the effect. Dark surfaces such as asphalt roads and roofs absorb large amounts of solar energy during the day and release it slowly at night. Buildings block the wind and trap warm air between them. Cities also have far fewer trees and plants than rural areas, and therefore lose the cooling effect produced when water evaporates from leaves. Finally, cars, factories and air conditioners all release heat directly into the urban environment.

C The difference in temperature is usually greatest at night, not during the day. In the countryside, the ground cools quickly after sunset, but in cities the stored heat keeps temperatures high. This matters because the human body needs cool nights to recover from hot days. During heatwaves, the number of deaths in cities often rises sharply, especially among elderly people who live alone.

D Air conditioning offers relief to those who can afford it, but Dr Lucas Ferreira warns that it can also make the problem worse. Air conditioners move heat from inside buildings to the street outside, and the electricity they use is often generated by burning fossil fuels. The result, he argues, is a cycle in which hotter cities lead to more air conditioning, which in turn makes cities hotter still.

E Several cheaper solutions have been tested. Dr Priya Nair has measured the effect of painting roofs white or covering them with reflective materials. Her studies found that such 'cool roofs' can reduce the temperature inside top-floor rooms by several degrees and lower the energy needed for cooling. Professor Erik Holm has focused on trees, which provide both shade and evaporative cooling. In his research, streets with a continuous tree canopy were considerably cooler at pedestrian level than similar streets without trees, though he notes that trees need water and years of care before they are fully effective.

F Some cities are now including heat in their long-term planning. Dr Yuki Tanaka has studied cities that keep open 'wind corridors' – lines of parks, rivers and low buildings that allow cooler air to flow into the centre. She argues that such decisions must be made early, because once a district has been densely built, it is very difficult and expensive to change. In her view, protecting green space should be treated not as a luxury but as essential infrastructure.

Can Money Buy Happiness?

The question of whether money makes people happy is as old as money itself, but only in recent decades have researchers tried to answer it with data. In the 1970s, the economist Richard Easterlin noticed a puzzle. Within any single country, richer people tended to report being happier than poorer people. Yet as whole countries became richer over time, their average level of happiness did not seem to rise. This observation became known as the Easterlin paradox, and it has been debated ever since.

One explanation is that people judge their income by comparing it with others. If everyone's income rises by the same amount, nobody feels better off relative to their neighbours, and so happiness stays the same. Another explanation is adaptation: people quickly become used to a higher standard of living, and what once felt like a luxury soon feels normal. A pay rise may produce a burst of satisfaction, but within months the effect fades.

Researchers have also distinguished between two kinds of happiness. The first is life satisfaction – how people evaluate their lives when asked to think about them as a whole. The second is emotional well-being – how often people feel joy, stress or sadness in their daily lives. A widely discussed study published in 2010 suggested that, while life satisfaction continued to rise with income, emotional well-being stopped improving above a certain level. Later research, using a different method, found that emotional well-being also continued to rise with income for most people. The debate has not been fully settled, but most researchers now agree that more money is associated with greater happiness, though each additional dollar matters less the richer a person already is.

Perhaps more useful than the question of how much money people have is the question of how they spend it. Several studies suggest that spending on experiences, such as travel or concerts, produces more lasting happiness than spending on material goods. Experiences are often shared with others, they become part of our personal stories, and they are less easily compared with what other people have. In one well-known experiment, participants who were given a small sum of money and told to spend it on someone else reported feeling happier at the end of the day than those told to spend it on themselves.

Another line of research focuses on time. People who use money to free up time – for example, by paying for services that save them from tasks they dislike – tend to report higher well-being. Yet many people, even those who can afford it, are reluctant to spend money in this way, perhaps because they feel they ought to do such tasks themselves.

In my view, these findings have a clear message for individuals and governments alike. For people living in poverty, more money makes an enormous difference, and policies that raise the incomes of the poorest are likely to do far more for overall happiness than those that make the rich richer. For those whose basic needs are already met, however, the way money is used may matter more than the amount. Relationships, health and a sense of purpose are not things that money can buy directly, but it can create the time and conditions in which they are more likely to grow.
