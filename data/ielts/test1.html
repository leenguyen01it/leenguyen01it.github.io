The Return of the Bicycle

For much of the twentieth century, the bicycle was the most common form of transport in many Asian cities. In the 1980s, the streets of Beijing and Hanoi were filled with millions of cyclists travelling to work, to school and to market. As incomes rose, however, bicycles were replaced first by motorbikes and then by cars, and in many places cycling came to be seen as a sign of poverty rather than a sensible choice.

In Europe, a similar decline had taken place earlier. After the Second World War, cities such as Amsterdam and Copenhagen were redesigned around the private car. Wide roads were built through historic neighbourhoods, and the number of cyclists fell year after year. By the early 1970s, the human cost of this change had become impossible to ignore. In the Netherlands alone, more than 3,000 people were killed on the roads in 1971, around 400 of them children. Public protests, led by a movement whose name translates as 'Stop the Child Murder', put pressure on the government to make streets safer. The oil crisis of 1973, which made fuel expensive almost overnight, strengthened the argument for cycling.

The result was a long period of investment. Today, Dutch cities have thousands of kilometres of cycle paths that are physically separated from motor traffic, and in Amsterdam, a greater share of journeys in the city centre is made by bicycle than by car. Cycling there is not seen as a sport or a hobby, but simply as the most practical way to get around.

The recent return of the bicycle in other parts of the world has been driven by different concerns. Air pollution, traffic congestion and rising rates of obesity have encouraged city governments to look again at two wheels. One of the most visible results has been the spread of public bike-sharing schemes. The scheme launched in Paris in 2007 was not the first of its kind, but its size – thousands of bicycles available at hundreds of stations – showed that bike-sharing could work in a major city.

A decade later, a new model appeared in China. Using smartphone apps, riders could unlock a bicycle and leave it anywhere when they had finished, without returning it to a station. Investors poured money into these 'dockless' companies, which competed by flooding cities with far more bicycles than people could use. Within a few years, many of the companies had collapsed, leaving huge piles of abandoned bicycles at the edges of cities.

Perhaps the most important change, however, has been the electric bicycle. A small motor that assists the rider makes hills and long distances much easier to manage, and allows people to arrive at work without being exhausted. Electric bicycles have proved especially popular with older riders, many of whom had given up cycling altogether.

Critics argue that cycling only suits cities that are flat and have a mild climate. In hot and humid cities, they say, few people will want to ride to work. Supporters respond that electric assistance reduces the effort required, that shade trees planted along roads can make a large difference to comfort, and that employers can provide showers for staff. They point out that some of the world's busiest cycling cities have cold, wet winters.

The biggest barrier, though, is not the weather but safety. Surveys in many countries have found that fear of traffic is the main reason people give for not cycling. Researchers have also found that painted lanes on busy roads do little to change this. Physically separated lanes, on the other hand, attract many more new cyclists, particularly women and older people.

It is unlikely that the bicycle will replace the car completely. But in an age of crowded cities and rising concern about health and the environment, it is no longer seen as a symbol of the past. In many places, it is increasingly seen as part of the future.

Sleep and Memory

A Humans spend roughly a third of their lives asleep. For centuries, sleep was regarded as a passive state, a time when the brain simply switched off to rest. Over the past hundred years, however, researchers have discovered that the sleeping brain is remarkably busy, and that much of this activity appears to be connected with memory.

B One of the earliest studies of the subject was published in 1924. Two American psychologists asked participants to learn lists of nonsense syllables, and then tested them after periods of either sleep or ordinary waking activity. Those who had slept remembered considerably more. At the time, the researchers concluded that sleep did not actively improve memory. Instead, they argued, it simply protected newly learned information from interference by the many other experiences of waking life.

C Later research revealed that sleep is not a single state. A typical night consists of several cycles, each lasting around 90 minutes. Within each cycle, the sleeper passes through lighter stages into deep, slow-wave sleep, and then into rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, the stage most closely associated with vivid dreams. Deep sleep dominates the early part of the night, while REM sleep becomes longer towards the morning.

D A breakthrough came from studies of rats. Scientists recorded the activity of individual brain cells while the animals learned to find their way through a maze. When the rats later fell asleep, the same cells fired again in the same order, as if the animals were running through the maze in their minds. This 'replay' happened much faster than the original experience, and it suggested that the sleeping brain was rehearsing what it had learned during the day.

E It now appears that different types of memory depend on different stages of sleep. Memory for facts, such as names, dates and vocabulary, seems to benefit most from deep sleep. Skills, such as playing a musical instrument or typing, appear to be strengthened mainly during the later stages of the night. In one experiment, participants who practised a finger-tapping sequence in the evening performed it faster and more accurately the next morning, without any further practice.

F These findings have practical consequences. Students who stay awake all night before an examination may remember less, not more, because they lose the stage of sleep in which new facts are strengthened. Short daytime naps can also help. In a study led by Dr Hana Okafor, office workers who took a 60-minute nap after a training session recalled significantly more of the material that afternoon than colleagues who had stayed awake.

G Many questions remain unanswered. Scientists still disagree about whether sleep actively strengthens memories or mainly prevents them from fading, and little is known about why sleep becomes lighter and more broken in old age. Meanwhile, a growing industry sells devices and apps that claim to improve sleep and memory. Researchers warn that few of these products have been properly tested, and that consumers should treat their claims with caution.

The Economics of Open-Source Software

Much of the digital world runs on software that nobody pays for. The operating systems on most web servers, the programming languages used by millions of developers and many of the tools that keep the internet working are 'open source': their code is published freely, and anyone may use, study or change it. To economists, this has long been a puzzle. Why would highly skilled people spend their time producing something valuable and then give it away?

One explanation focuses on careers. Professor Mark Delaney argues that contributions to well-known projects work as a kind of public advertisement. Unlike a CV, which an employer must take on trust, code that has been reviewed and accepted by other experts provides visible evidence of ability. Delaney's research suggests that developers with a strong record of open-source contributions receive more job offers and, on average, higher salaries.

Dr Sofia Reyes accepts that career benefits matter, but believes they are only part of the story. In her interviews with hundreds of contributors, the most common reason given was simply that programmers enjoy solving problems. Many projects, she found, began because a developer needed a tool that did not exist and decided to build it. Having done so, sharing it cost almost nothing, and other people's improvements made it better.

The picture of the lone volunteer, however, is increasingly out of date. A large proportion of the code in major projects is now written by people who are paid by their employers to do so. Dr Kenji Morita has studied why companies, many of them direct competitors, cooperate in this way. He found that firms contribute partly to share the cost of maintaining software that they all depend on, but above all to influence how it develops. A company that helps to shape a widely used technical standard is in a stronger position than one that must simply accept decisions made by others.

Not all projects benefit from this kind of support. Dr Amara Osei has drawn attention to what she calls the 'maintenance gap'. Some pieces of software are used by thousands of organisations but are looked after by one or two unpaid volunteers. When those individuals become exhausted or lose interest, the consequences can be serious. In 2014, a major security flaw was discovered in a widely used encryption library that had been maintained for years by a very small team with almost no funding. The problem affected a large part of the internet.

Measuring the value of open-source software is also difficult. Because it is free, it does not appear in the figures that governments use to calculate economic output. Professor Lena Fischer has attempted to estimate what it would cost companies to rewrite the open-source software they use if it suddenly disappeared. Her estimate runs into trillions of dollars, a figure that, she argues, shows how much standard economic statistics fail to capture.

Various ways of funding open-source work have been tried, from individual donations to foundations supported by large technology companies. In my view, governments should go further and treat the most critical projects as public infrastructure, in the same way as roads or bridges. However, money alone will not solve the problem. Many projects succeed because of a culture of openness, generosity and shared standards, and badly designed funding could damage that culture as easily as it supports it. The challenge is to provide stability without turning a community into a bureaucracy.
